Overview

Project question

How did airport traffic and security evolve after the terrorist attacks on September 11, 2001, in the United States?

The project compares airport traffic from a few years before 9/11 with traffic a few years after. It also looks at airport-related text to see whether security-related language became more visible over time. The expectation is that security would receive more attention after 9/11, while traffic would drop immediately after the attacks before later recovering.

Traffic analysis

Airports are treated as nodes in a network, while flights between airports are treated as weighted edges. A higher edge weight means more flights between connected airports.

Text analysis

Airport-related text was gathered from Wikipedia and analyzed using tokenization, TF-IDF scores, and word clouds to identify important and frequent terms.

Among the eight recurring major hubs, flight strength increased by 7.06% from 1999–2000 to 2002–2003. At the same time, average departure delay, arrival delay, cancellations, and diversions decreased in this selected comparison.

These results should not be interpreted as proof that the entire U.S. aviation system became more efficient. Looking at a longer period from 1988 to 2007 shows a large drop in flights after 9/11, followed by recovery and continued growth.

Network visualizations

Airport network maps

The two network visualizations show airport communities and traffic intensity. Warmer connections indicate more flights, and labeled nodes mark the ten most visited airports in each period.

The 1999–2000 network appears more spread out, while the 2002–2003 network places the strongest airports more centrally. The later period also contains more nodes, suggesting that more airports were included in the network.

Word cloud visualizations

The word clouds show the most frequent words in the 2003 and 2010 documents. Larger words appear more often in the text.

Security and safety-related words are not dominant in either word cloud. In 2003, the term “crashed” appears frequently, while the 2010 word cloud includes terms such as “World War” and “War.” Because word clouds do not show context, these terms cannot be directly interpreted as evidence of airport security changes.

Discussion

What the results suggest

The traffic results suggest a short-term disruption after 9/11, followed by recovery. In the recurring top-airport comparison, traffic increased while delays, cancellations, and diversions decreased. However, the long-term graph shows that cancellations later rose again, so the improvement in the selected-airport table should not be treated as a permanent trend for the whole aviation system.

The text analysis suggests that security-related language became more visible in later airport-related texts. Still, the large difference in document length between 2003 and 2010 makes the comparison uneven. The text results are therefore best understood as supporting evidence rather than a final conclusion.

Conclusion

Conclusion

Overall, the project shows that U.S. airport traffic was strongly affected by 9/11 but recovered in the following years. Among the recurring major hubs, traffic was higher in 2002–2003 than in 1999–2000, while several operational delay and cancellation measures were lower.

The text analysis indicates that airport-related writing became more likely to include security-related terms over time. Because the available text becomes much longer in later years, this result should be interpreted carefully. Together, the network and text analyses suggest that aviation recovered after 9/11 while security became a more visible part of airport discussion.

  • Traffic recovered after the initial drop
  • Recurring major hubs increased in strength
  • Security language became more visible
  • Text results need cautious interpretation
